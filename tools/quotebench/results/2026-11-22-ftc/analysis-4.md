# How the buying-group agreement was proved, or not, against each distributor

*In the Matter of Benco Dental Supply Co., Henry Schein, Inc., and Patterson Companies, Inc.*, FTC Docket No. 9379. This is a working analysis for the partner meeting. Each quotation below is drawn from the filing, transcript or decision cited after it, by file name. Where a point is my own reading rather than what a document says, it is marked **[Inference]**.

## What this is built from, and what it is not

- **Read:** the Initial Decision (Oct. 15, 2019); complaint counsel's post-trial brief and reply brief; Benco's post-trial brief and reply; Schein's post-trial brief and reply; Patterson's post-hearing brief and reply (with each side's proposed findings used only to pin down an exhibit's wording); the respondents' joint post-trial findings and conclusions on the legal standard; and trial transcript volumes 17–20.
- **Not in this matter:** the trial exhibits themselves (the CX/RX emails, texts, notes and investigational-hearing transcripts). Every exhibit below is quoted **as a filing or the decision quotes it**, and is identified by its exhibit number so you can request it. File names cited are the filings that quote it.

They hold the last defense witnesses (economists Johnson, Wu and Carlton; Smile Source's Maurer; Schein's Titus and Cavaretta; Klear Impakt's Dr. Richard Johnson). **Cohen, Guggenheim, Sullivan, Ryan, Foley and Misiak do not testify in these volumes**; their testimony appears here only as the briefs and the decision quote it (e.g. Cohen at Tr. 501). ## The legal standard each side argued, and the one the ALJ applied

- **Complaint counsel** framed its case as direct evidence: inter-firm communications (exchanges of assurances, confrontations about deviations, reassurances), not parallel conduct :cite{#ccb-40}. It argued the parallel-conduct-plus-factors test does not apply to such a case: :q{#ccb-94}
- **Respondents** (jointly and separately) argued the Matsushita/Monsanto line: evidence must tend to exclude the possibility of independent action, ambiguous evidence supports only limited inferences, and the Williamson Oil three-step test governs :cite{#benb-13} :cite{#benb-63} :cite{#schb-44}. Patterson put it most strongly: :q{#patb-66} Respondents also treated the witnesses' sworn denials as direct evidence of no agreement :cite{#schb-46} :cite{#patb-38}.
- **The ALJ** applied a totality-of-the-evidence test :cite{#idbp-3} and held complaint counsel need not disprove every innocent explanation: :q{#idbp-4} He rejected the argument that parallel conduct plus plus factors is the only route to an agreement :cite{#idbp-6}.

**[Inference]** The standard did not decide the result by itself: the same standard produced liability for Patterson and dismissal for Schein. What differed was the content of each set of communications and each firm's conduct afterwards.

## 1. Benco: the policy at the center

### The evidence complaint counsel relied on

Benco's policy was not in dispute; what was disputed was whether Benco used it to secure its rivals' commitments. The decision records the policy and its enforcement:

::q{#idbp-9}

::q{#idbp-11}

(CX1038, Mar. 24, 2011, as quoted in the decision.) Complaint counsel's theory was that Benco, having adopted the policy unilaterally, feared it was futile unless its two largest rivals followed, and communicated with each to ensure they did :cite{#idbp-7}. Against Benco's claim that it learned of its rivals' policies from the market, complaint counsel quoted Cohen tying his understanding of Schein's policy to his texts with Sullivan: :q{#ccb-43}

### How Benco explained its conduct as its own decision

Benco called it a long-standing no-middleman policy, adopted unilaterally more than a decade before the alleged conspiracy:

::q{#benb-1}

Its stated business reason was compliance, meaning a group's ability to commit its members' purchasing volume :cite{#benb-6}. Its economist gave the same rationale at trial :cite{#trx-2}. Benco argued that no evidence showed it asked anyone to do anything :cite{#idbp-13}.

### How the ALJ weighed it

The ALJ accepted that the policy predated the alleged conspiracy, but held that this was no defense: an agreement to adhere to an existing course of conduct is still an agreement :q{#idbp-48}. Complaint counsel had made the same argument against Benco :cite{#ccb-100}. Benco's liability therefore ran entirely through the Patterson communications (Part 2). The Schein communications did not establish an agreement (Part 3).

## 4. Why it came out differently for Schein

What follows are the ALJ's stated reasons, each with its passage. The last point is my synthesis.

1. **What the other side said back.** For Patterson, Guggenheim answered Cohen and both executives agreed at trial on what the answer meant :cite{#idbp-28}. For Schein, the evidence on whether Cohen ever told Sullivan of the policy was conflicting, and no Sullivan reply disclosed a Schein position :cite{#idbs-2} :cite{#idbs-6}.
2. **Who started the contact, and the response.** Guggenheim initiated the June 2013 inquiry, and the ALJ read it as a request for reassurance :cite{#idbp-41b}. Every Benco–Schein contact was unsolicited by Schein. Sullivan and Foley rebuffed the contacts, and the ALJ credited them :cite{#idbs-29} :cite{#idbs-33} :cite{#idbs-42}. Schein's mirror-image argument made exactly this contrast :cite{#schb-65}.
3. **Conduct afterwards.** Patterson's conduct after February 2013 was a near-blanket refusal without evaluation, and its explanations were not supported by contemporaneous documents :cite{#idbp-58} :cite{#idbp-64}. Schein kept, added and bid for buying groups throughout the period :cite{#idbs-20} :cite{#idbs-45}.
4. **The same kind of evidence was weighed differently because the foundation differed.** The ALJ dismissed complaint counsel's confrontation and cheating readings for Schein because they presume prior assurances that were not proved :cite{#idbs-36} :cite{#idbs-23}. For Patterson, the February exchange supplied that foundation :cite{#idbp-42}.
5. **[Inference]** Schein's case turned largely on credibility: the ALJ credited Sullivan's and Foley's accounts of rebuffing Benco. In Patterson's case, the contemporaneous emails left little room for a contrary account. On any appeal, the Schein dismissal would rest substantially on those credibility findings, and the Patterson liability on the text of the two email chains.
