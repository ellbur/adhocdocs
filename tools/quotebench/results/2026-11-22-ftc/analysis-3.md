# How the buying-group agreement was proved, or not, against each distributor

*In the Matter of Benco Dental Supply Co., Henry Schein, Inc. and Patterson Companies, Inc., FTC Docket No. 9379. Prepared for the partner meeting. Each quotation below is drawn from the cited filing; the citation gives its file name. My own reading is marked **Inference**.*

## What this analysis is built from, and what it is not

The matter holds 91 public docket filings: the pleadings, motions and orders, the parties' pre- and post-trial briefs, proposed findings and replies, the Initial Decision, and four trial transcript volumes (Vols. 17–20, Tr. 4772–5670). It does **not** hold the trial exhibits themselves (the CX/RX emails and texts) or the transcripts of the witnesses at the center of the case: Cohen, Ryan, Guggenheim, Misiak, Rogan, McFadden, Sullivan, Foley and Steck all testified in volumes not in the matter. So where I quote an email or a piece of testimony, I quote it **as a filing quotes it**, and I cite that filing. The exhibit numbers given in the briefs (for example CX0056, CX0090) are noted so the underlying documents can be pulled from the record. Where the filings quote the same document differently, I say so.

The four transcript volumes we have cover respondents' later witnesses: Smile Source's Trevor Maurer, Schein's Kathleen Titus and Joe Cavaretta, and the experts Wu, Carlton and Johnson. They matter mostly to the Schein side, and I use them there.

I read the Initial Decision's Analysis (Part II) in full; complaint counsel's post-trial brief (facts, and the rebuttal on Schein); Benco's, Patterson's and Schein's post-trial briefs on the agreement issues; Patterson's reply brief on the internal documents; complaint counsel's reply brief on Sullivan's credibility and Foley; and the relevant passages of transcript Vols. 17, 18 and 20. I did not read the proposed findings and replies to findings in full, nor the parts of the briefs on market definition, harm, remedy and the Burkhart invitation-to-collude count. Nothing in the matter postdates the Initial Decision, apart from three procedural filings and transcript re-releases dated 2020; any appeal to the Commission is not in it.

## The bottom line

The ALJ (Chief Judge Chappell, October 15, 2019) found :q{#id-outcome-bp} On Schein: :q{#id-outcome-s}

The difference came down to one thing. For Patterson there was a written exchange between the two top executives: Cohen stated Benco's policy and Guggenheim answered. Everything else, the NMDC withdrawal, the June 2013 "confrontation" and Patterson's internal instructions, was read in that light. For Schein the ALJ found no such starting exchange. Without it, Benco's later contacts could not be treated as enforcement or reassurance of an agreement, and Schein's actual dealings with buying groups pointed the other way. The ALJ put it this way: :q{#id-weaker}

## The legal frame the ALJ applied

Three rulings shaped how each piece of evidence was weighed:

- **Burden.** Preponderance, with the inference of agreement required to beat independent action: :q{#id-standard}
- **No parallel-conduct-plus-factors requirement.** Respondents argued that a circumstantial case needs parallel conduct and "plus factors" (Williamson Oil). The ALJ disagreed: :q{#id-plusfactors-rejected} This helped complaint counsel against Patterson. Against Schein it did not matter, because the ALJ went on to find no parallel conduct and no plus factors in any event.
- **Confrontation and reassurance need a prior agreement.** Evidence that one competitor confronted another about "cheating," or reassured it, counts as assent only if there is something to cheat on. That ruling decided most of the Schein evidence (below).

## Benco

Benco is liable only because of its agreement with Patterson. The ALJ made no separate finding that Benco joined anything with Schein.

**Complaint counsel's theory.** Benco was the hub. :q{#ccb-open-relationship} The agreement, it argued, ran through the top executives: :q{#ccb-intro-theory}

**Benco's explanation.** Its conduct was a long-standing, unilateral business policy: :q{#bb-story} It was open about the policy: :q{#bb-notsecret} And it never asked anyone to do anything: :q{#bb-missing}

**How the ALJ weighed it.** The policy itself was not in dispute: :q{#id-benco-undisputed} The question was why Cohen told his competitor about it. The ALJ found :q{#id-benco-notpersuasive} He also turned Benco's "public knowledge" defense around: :q{#id-public-knowledge} As to Schein, Benco's repeated outreach was troubling but not enough: :q{#id-benco-concerns}

**Inference.** Benco lost on its communications with Patterson, not on its policy. The ALJ accepted that Benco had a long-standing unilateral policy. What made it an agreement was telling a rival's president about it and getting an answer.

## Why it came out differently for Schein

The Initial Decision gives these reasons, in this order of weight:

1. **No starting exchange.** For Patterson there was a written statement of Benco's policy and a written reply. For Schein there was only Cohen's uncorroborated "I believe I did," contradicted by Sullivan, with no date and no document (:cite{#id-weaker}).
2. **Who reached out, and how the other side responded.** Guggenheim *initiated* the June 2013 inquiry and accepted Cohen's explanation. Every Benco–Schein contact was Benco's, and the ALJ credited Sullivan's and Foley's testimony that they cut it off, supported by Foley's own contemporaneous note. The ALJ said so directly about the ADC communications: :q{#id-parallel35}
3. **Confrontation and reassurance presuppose an agreement.** Without the 2011 exchange, Benco's later contacts could not be read as enforcing anything (:cite{#id-no-prior-reassure}). The ALJ thought it at least as likely that Benco was trying, and failing, to persuade Schein (:cite{#id-persuade}).
4. **Conduct.** Schein kept, added and bid for buying groups throughout 2011–2015 (MeritDent, Schulman, Dental Gator, Klear Impakt, the 2014 Smile Source bid and the Kois talks), while Benco and Patterson refused the same groups. Complaint counsel's "cheating" answer was rejected as assuming the agreement (:cite{#id-cheating}).
5. **The same kinds of evidence were weighed differently by company.** Denials were given "little weight" against Patterson because documents contradicted them. For Schein the denials were consistent with Schein's conduct. Internal "everyone says no" statements (Misiak, Rogan, Ryan, Foley) were credited as instructions to Patterson's own staff but discounted as evidence of what Schein was doing.

**Inference.** Two points of vulnerability for the Schein dismissal, if it is being reviewed. First, it rests heavily on credibility: Sullivan over Cohen on the 2011 disclosure, and Sullivan's rebuff over his own contemporaneous texts. Complaint counsel's reply brief makes the strongest version of that attack (Sullivan's changed testimony, and his failure to report the contacts under Schein's antitrust policy). Second, the ALJ conceded that Schein had not explained away all of its internal statements, and his finding that Schein had no policy rests on conduct evidence that complaint counsel says includes non-buying groups and legacy accounts. Weighing that conduct turns on the definition of "buying group," which the parties never agreed on. Equally, the Patterson finding's weakest link is the inferred instruction behind the NMDC withdrawal (Part 2 above).
